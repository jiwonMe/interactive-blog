# The Secretary Problem

You are the HR lead who must interview 100 candidates **one by one**. After each interview you must **decide immediately**. A rejected candidate cannot return. The goal is to hire the single best person.

What should you do?

## The 37% (1/e) rule

The mathematically proven answer:

1. **Observe** the first 37% of candidates without hiring anyone.
2. **Select** the first candidate who is better than everyone you have seen so far.

Following that rule yields a success probability of roughly **37%**, far better than a random pick.

## Why 37%?

If there are $n$ candidates and you only observe the first $r$, the success probability is

$$
P(r) = \frac{r}{n} \sum_{i=r+1}^{n} \frac{1}{i-1}
$$

Maximizing the expression leads to $r/n = 1/e \approx 0.368$ as $n \to \infty$.

## Real-life applications

1. **Hiring**: design interview flows and decision checkpoints.
2. **Real estate**: decide when to stop touring and make an offer.
3. **Dating**: determine when to commit.
4. **Trading**: pick buy/sell moments.
5. **Parking**: skip the first few spots and then take the first good one.

## Variants

1. **Multiple winners**: what if you can hire the top $k$ candidates?
2. **Recallable candidates**: can you invite people back later?
3. **Noisy rankings**: what if your evaluations contain measurement error?

## Key insights

> **Even with incomplete information, a structured policy can approximate the optimal outcome.**

1. **Exploration**: observe enough samples to build a baseline.
2. **Commitment**: once you have a baseline, act decisively.
3. **Balance**: neither rush nor wait forever.

## Mathematical beauty

The fact that the answer is tied to the natural constant $e$ is striking. The same constant appears in compound interest, probability distributions, and natural growth, hinting that optimal stopping is deeply connected to broader laws of nature.

## Bonus hiring drill

Set the simulator to 40 candidates:

1. Observe the first 14 candidates (≈35%).
2. Record the highest score during exploration.
3. Starting with candidate 15, hire the first person who beats that benchmark.

Repeat the exercise a few times—your intuition for where the cutoff should be will quickly align with the math.
